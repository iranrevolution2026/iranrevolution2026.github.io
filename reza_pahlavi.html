Who is Reza Pahlavi & Why Iranians Support Him?

From Exiled Crown Prince to Transitional Figure

Reza Pahlavi is the son of Iran’s last monarch, Mohammad Reza Shah Pahlavi. In 1979, as the Islamic Revolution swept his father from power, the 19-year-old crown prince was in the United States undergoing pilot training. He has lived in exile for nearly five decades since the fall of the monarchy. Unlike many heirs of deposed dynasties who retreat into private life, Pahlavi chose a different path: he devoted his adult life to opposing the Islamic Republic and to the arduous task of building unity among Iran’s fragmented opposition.

Over the years, he has sought to bring together monarchists and republicans, liberals and leftists, secular activists and religious dissidents—an unlikely coalition in a political culture fractured by decades of repression and mutual distrust. Based largely in North America and Europe, he has consistently lobbied Western governments against the Islamic Republic’s human rights abuses and regional militarism, including advocating for stronger international measures against the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC).

As the Islamic Republic has pursued increasingly radical policies—producing economic collapse, social repression, environmental degradation, and regional confrontation—Pahlavi has come to represent, for many Iranians, not a return to monarchy but what some describe as an “experienced future”: a vision rooted in Iran’s pre-1979 trajectory of state-building, social modernization, and engagement with the world. His grandfather, Reza Shah, unified a fractured state and built foundational institutions of modern governance; his father presided over rapid socio-economic development, expanded education, women’s suffrage, and secular public order.

Today, Pahlavi presents himself not as a ruler-in-waiting, but as a transitional figure committed to popular sovereignty. He repeatedly emphasizes that Iran’s future system of government—monarchy or republic—must be decided by Iranians through a free vote. The nationwide response to his calls for demonstrations, along with visible support among Iranians inside the country and across the diaspora, suggests that he has emerged as the most recognizable opposition figure around whom disparate forces might cohere in a transition away from the Islamic Republic toward a secular, democratic order grounded in the will of the people.

Why Iranians Are Uniting Behind Reza Pahlavi

  1. He Represents an “Experienced Future,” Not Simple Nostalgia

    Support for Pahlavi is often dismissed as nostalgia for monarchy. Yet for many Iranians, the appeal lies less in restoring the past than in reclaiming a developmental trajectory interrupted in 1979. The pre-revolutionary period is remembered—especially in contrast to today’s crises—for economic growth, institution-building, expanded education, women’s enfranchisement, and a relatively normal place for Iran in the international system.

  2. Consistent Messaging in a Landscape of Failed Reform

    For nearly five decades, Pahlavi’s core message has remained consistent: the Islamic Republic is structurally irreformable, and Iran’s crisis cannot be resolved through cosmetic change within the system. This stance has gained credibility as successive waves of “reformists” failed to curb repression or prevent mass killings of protesters.

  3. Lowering the Cost of Coordination in a Fragmented Opposition

    Iran’s opposition has long suffered from debilitating fragmentation—ideological rivalries, ethnic divides, and personal feuds that raise the cost of collective action. Pahlavi’s name has functioned as a focal point in this fragmented landscape.

  4. Exhaustion with Radical Politics and Fear of Chaotic Rupture

    Iranians are weary of revolutionary maximalism that promises salvation and delivers catastrophe. Yet they also recognize that dismantling a regime sustained by executions, torture, and coercion cannot be achieved through incremental reform alone.

  5. A Transitional Plan Anchored in Popular Sovereignty

    In July 2025, Pahlavi published an “Emergency Phase” booklet to articulate a procedural vision for transition. His proposed “Emergency Phase” framework outlines interim governance mechanisms, stabilization priorities, and a pathway toward free elections and a constituent process.
