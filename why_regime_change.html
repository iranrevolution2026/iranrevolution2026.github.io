Why Iranians Want Regime Change

The Iranian People’s Long Road to Liberation: 47 Years of Resistance

For the past 47 years, Iranians have been living through a nonstop cycle of repression, resistance, and survival. Since the Islamic Republic took power after the overthrow of the Shah in 1979, the country has been ruled by a system that not only wages war on its own people but also acts as a major state sponsor of terrorism. Inside Iran, dissent is crushed through violence and fear. Outside Iran, the same regime targets Israel, Western countries, and U.S. interests, making Iran’s internal struggle directly connected to American security and the stability of the Middle East.

Right after seizing power, the Islamic Republic moved quickly to lock in clerical control. Political rivals and independent institutions were eliminated, revolutionary courts were established, and the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) was created as the regime’s main tool of repression. The 1980s were marked by systematic executions, torture, and brutal prison conditions, culminating in the 1988 mass execution of political prisoners. Fear became an official policy. At the same time, the regime began exporting violence beyond Iran’s borders by building proxy militias and terrorist networks. The IRGC plays a dual role: suppressing Iranian citizens at home while attacking U.S. forces and allies abroad, all under the same ideology and command structure. This includes long-term sponsorship of groups such as Hamas, Hezbollah, Hashd al-Shaabi, and the Houthis, most recently seen in Hamas’s October 7 attack on Israeli civilians, which was funded and supported by the Islamic Republic.

Resistance has never stopped. Major protest movements erupted in 1999, 2009 (Green Movement), 2017–2018, 2019 (Bloody Aban), 2022–2023 (Women, Life, Freedom), and most recently the 2025–2026 nationwide revolution. Every time, the response has been the same: escalating lethal force by directly shooting at people, mass arrests, and executions used as a way to scare the population into silence.

For years, many Iranians tried reform from within—through elections, civil activism, and reformist promises—but those hopes collapsed. Reformist leaders wouldn’t confront state violence, leading to widespread loss of trust and a growing understanding that the system itself is the problem. Women, young people, and religious and ethnic minorities have been at the center of resistance.

  • Women face gender apartheid through mandatory hijab> laws and morality police crackdowns.
  • Social freedoms are heavily restricted, and everyday activities such as dancing in public, holding mixed-gender parties, or organizing events where women are not wearing hijab can result in arrests or the shutdown of cafes, restaurants, and public places.
  • Religious and ethnic minorities are disproportionately targeted, with the Baha’i community facing systematic persecution and restrictions on basic rights such as education and owning a business. Ethnic minorities, including Kurds and Baluchis, experience disproportionate executions and unequal investment in their regions.
  • Cultural expression is tightly controlled, with constant surveillance of musicians, actors, artists, theatrical works, and other creative content.
  • Political activism is also constantly suppressed through routine imprisonment and torture.

State-sponsored crimes have taken place both inside and outside Iran. Inside the country, the regime is responsible for tragedies such as the downing of Ukraine International Airlines Flight 752 in January 2019 . Outside Iran, the regime has carried out assassinations and intimidation campaigns, including the murder of Iranian dissident artist Fereydoun Farrokhzad , and has built terror networks in Europe and North America to threaten, attack, or abduct political dissidents abroad.

Environmental destruction has added another layer of suffering. Decades of mismanagement have dried up lakes and rivers, causing water shortages for industry, farming, and everyday life. Crops have failed, farmers have lost their livelihoods, and several animal species have gone extinct or are close to extinction, including the iconic Iranian cheetah. These crises are not natural disasters, but they are the result of corruption and neglect.

Economically, the country is in deep trouble. Inflation continues to rise, poverty is widespread, and the IRGC dominates a large portion of the economy, pushing out independent private businesses. Corruption is systemic. The IRGC and its affiliates profit from sanctions through black markets and smuggling, while ordinary people struggle to survive. State revenue is diverted away from public needs and instead spent on sponsoring terrorism abroad or projects like investing in state-run supermarkets in Venezuela. Inside Iran, shortages of basic necessities, including electricity, water, and medicine, are common. Human rights violations remain widespread, with high numbers of political prisoners and executions, and the country suffers from massive brain drain as students and professionals leave and never return.

All of this history shows why Iranians are no longer asking for reform within the system. After nearly five decades of repression, corruption, and broken promises, they are demanding the total dismantlement of the Islamic Republic and a full transition to a free, secular, and democratic society.
