Iran’s New Revolution: What Happened?

Starting December 28, 2025, Tehran’s merchants and shopkeepers went on strike over the collapse of Iran’s currency, sparking nationwide protests fueled by anger at decades of repression and economic mismanagement. Rising prices drove more people to join strikes and demonstrations. On January 6, Reza Pahlavi, son of Iran’s former king, called for nationwide demonstrations on January 8–9, prompting millions to take to the streets across all 31 provinces, chanting slogans such as “Down with the dictator” and “This is the last battle; Pahlavi will return” demanding regime change and supporting Reza Pahlavi as the leading representative of the opposition to the Islamic Republic.

The Iranian government responded with a brutal crackdown: Security forces, including police, IRGC, disguised intelligence agents, and reportedly foreign proxy militias, used live ammunition, rifles, snipers, tear gas, and batons, targeting protesters in streets and hospitals. The government imposed a nationwide communications blackout, including internet, landline, and mobile communications. Reports indicate upwards of 30,000 people were killed in just two days, with thousands more injured, including many with severe eye injuries, and widespread arrests. This toll continues to rise, with media outlets updating the death count daily. Families were forced to pay “bullet money” for bodies; failure to pay resulted in mass graves.

Detainees face torture, execution, or poisoning, with some executed shortly after arrest. The protests were widespread, occurring in both major and small cities as well as rural areas, and the victims ranged from children to the elderly. Activists attempted to use the limited number of Starlink receivers in Iran to report events abroad, but security forces employed military-grade jammers and targeted the receivers for destruction whenever possible.

Regime propaganda seeks to portray protesters not as citizens but as terrorists or Mossad agents, or to downplay their demands by framing them as purely economic grievances. However, the protesters’ slogans, calling for the resignation of the regime’s leader and repeatedly chanting 'down with the dictator,' show that their demands go far beyond economic issues. The lists of those killed further illustrate that they were ordinary citizens, ranging from children to the elderly, students to shopkeepers, and professional musicians to athletes, demonstrating the broad-based nature of the movement. Moreover, these protests are consistent with demonstrations from three years ago, in which citizens emphasized social demands such as freedom, a dignified life, and the recognition of women’s rights. Taken together, it is clear that the people’s demands are comprehensive, targeting all elements of this oppressive regime, not just its economic policies.
